# Bayesian Optimization in Industrial Engineering: Application Guide

## First question: is each evaluation cheap or expensive?

Before choosing Bayesian Optimization, ask whether the performance of a candidate decision can be evaluated cheaply.

If the objective can be calculated directly with a formula, an LP/MILP model, or a fast specialized algorithm, Bayesian Optimization is usually not the first choice.

It becomes more attractive when each candidate requires:

- a long discrete-event simulation,
- a physical manufacturing experiment,
- an expensive engineering analysis,
- hours of machine-learning training,
- or a costly quality test.

## 1. Manufacturing process parameters

Possible decision variables include spindle speed, feed rate, depth of cut, welding current, furnace temperature, holding time, injection pressure, mold temperature, or cooling time.

Possible objectives include surface quality, cycle time, energy, scrap rate, or an explicit economic trade-off among them.

Bayesian Optimization is useful when physical experiments are expensive and the main goal is to locate a high-performing region with fewer sequential trials than a dense factorial search.

## 2. Simulation-based production-system optimization

Possible decisions include operators per station, number of machines, buffer capacity, batch size, vehicle count, maintenance thresholds, or dispatching-rule parameters.

Typical outputs are throughput, WIP, flow time, waiting time, and total operating cost. The objective may be generated by Arena, AnyLogic, SimPy, or a custom discrete-event simulation.

## 3. Maintenance optimization

Possible decisions include preventive-maintenance interval, condition-monitoring alarm threshold, spare-parts level, planned downtime, and maintenance-team capacity.

A possible objective is:

```text
expected failure cost
+ planned maintenance cost
+ production-loss cost
+ inventory cost
```

Bayesian Optimization is a good candidate when the reliability or simulation model is itself expensive to evaluate.

## 4. Inventory-policy tuning

Typical decision variables are reorder point `r`, order quantity `Q`, base-stock level `S`, or a safety-stock factor. With stochastic demand and lead time, policy performance can be estimated by Monte Carlo or discrete-event simulation.

A monetary objective can combine holding, ordering, stockout, and backorder costs.

## 5. Scheduling: where BO fits and where it does not

Using Gaussian Process BO directly to search the entire permutation space of a classical job-shop or flow-shop problem is usually not the natural first approach.

More suitable uses include tuning:

- mutation rate in a genetic algorithm,
- tabu tenure,
- weights of dispatching rules,
- parameters of a simulation-based scheduling policy,
- solver or heuristic hyperparameters.

In other words, BO often optimizes the **policy or solution method** around a combinatorial scheduling problem rather than replacing the scheduling method itself.

## 6. Quality engineering and design of experiments

When each physical experiment is expensive, Bayesian Optimization can be viewed as a sequential experimental-design strategy. A GP response surface is fitted after the initial runs, and an acquisition function selects the next experiment.

Bayesian Optimization does not make classical DOE obsolete. DOE remains valuable for estimating factor effects, analyzing interactions, and supporting statistical inference. A practical workflow can use DOE or Latin Hypercube Sampling for initial experiments and then continue with sequential Bayesian Optimization.

## 7. Energy efficiency

Potential decisions include machine speed, HVAC set points, furnace profiles, compressor pressure, and production rate. The goal may be to reduce energy while maintaining quality and throughput constraints. An expensive digital twin or physical test makes this a natural BO setting.

## 8. Relationship to classical Operations Research

| Problem | Typical first choice |
|---|---|
| Deterministic product-mix model | LP |
| Capacitated production planning | MILP |
| Small/medium constraint-based scheduling | CP-SAT |
| Production simulation taking 20 minutes per scenario | Consider Bayesian Optimization |
| Expensive physical CNC experiment | Strong Bayesian Optimization candidate |
| Expensive ML hyperparameter tuning | Strong Bayesian Optimization candidate |

Bayesian Optimization is not a universal substitute for LP, MILP, CP-SAT, Gurobi, Pyomo, or specialized combinatorial algorithms.

## 9. Dimensionality

Classical GP-based Bayesian Optimization is strongest in low and moderate dimensions. As the number of decision variables grows, GP fitting, kernel learning, and acquisition optimization become harder and the required experimental budget increases.

In high-dimensional spaces, alternatives such as TPE, random-forest surrogates, evolutionary methods, or problem-specific decompositions may be more suitable.

## 10. Project checklist

Before using Bayesian Optimization in an industrial-engineering project:

1. Define the objective precisely.
2. Identify continuous, integer, and categorical decisions.
3. Quantify the cost of one evaluation.
4. Identify sources of stochastic noise.
5. Define how constraints will be treated.
6. Express objective components in compatible units or state an explicit scalarization.
7. Validate the optimizer against exhaustive or random search on a small test problem.
8. Record seeds and replication design.
9. Validate the selected solution with independent simulation or experimental replications.
10. State clearly that standard Bayesian Optimization does not guarantee a global optimum.
